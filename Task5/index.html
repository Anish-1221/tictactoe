Keeping the search inside memory

A 10×10 board offers 100 opening moves, then 99, then 98. Searching even four plies ahead for three players means on the order of 108 positions, which no browser tab will survive. Three heuristics keep it tractable, and all three are exposed above so their effect can be seen.

Candidate restriction. Only empty cells within the candidate radius of an existing mark are considered. A move in open space far from every other mark cannot contribute to a line any time soon. On a 10×10 board this typically cuts the branching factor from around 90 to around 20. It is the single change that makes large boards work. It is a heuristic, not a sound reduction, so in principle it can overlook a good move in empty space.

Depth limiting with an evaluation function. Below the limit the search stops and scores the position directly: for each straight run of cells long enough to win, a player with marks in it and no opponent marks scores the cube of their count. Cubing rather than squaring makes near-complete lines dominate, which is what makes the engine respond to threats instead of spreading marks thinly.

Move ordering and immediate pruning. Near the root, candidates are tried best-first by that same evaluation, and a move that wins outright ends the search of its siblings, since nothing can score higher.

Where it runs out

Measured on this implementation, three players, candidate radius 2: depth 3 completes on every board size from 3×3 to 10×10 inside the default ceiling of 400,000 nodes per move, taking under a second per game. Depth 4 exceeds that ceiling from 6×6 upward, peaking at roughly 1.5 million nodes on a 6×6 board. Rather than letting the tab run out of memory, the search returns its best result so far and the run reports how many moves were cut short.
